FIELD LESSONS & ERP DESIGN

System Design Lessons From Ground-Level Logistics

Designing practical, ergonomic ERP software based on ground-level warehouse operations.

In the early years of building software systems, I believed that clarity came from leadership conversations. Meet the CEO. Understand the vision. Translate it into technology. That seemed logical.

But over time, working across manufacturing units, trading businesses, and operational-heavy environments, I realized something far more important: The real truth of a business does not live in presentations. It lives in execution. And execution is owned not by executives — but by operators. Warehouse managers. Plant supervisors. Field teams. Sales coordinators.

The people who actually make the business run every single day. Some of the most valuable lessons we’ve learned at Infipre IT Services didn’t come from strategy meetings. They came from listening carefully to people who were never part of those meetings.

The Day a Warehouse Operator Changed Our Approach

We were working on a warehouse management system for a trading business.

The leadership had clear expectations:

  • Real-time inventory tracking
  • Barcode-based inward and outward movement
  • Automated stock reconciliation
  • Dashboard visibility

Everything was aligned from a system design perspective. The workflows were structured. The modules were defined.

From a technical standpoint, it was a strong solution. Before finalizing the design, we decided to spend a day at the warehouse. Not in a conference room. On the actual floor. We observed how materials moved. How entries were made. How decisions were taken. At one point, we spoke to a warehouse operator responsible for dispatch.

We explained the barcode-based dispatch process. Scan item → validate → confirm → update stock. It was clean. Efficient. Technically correct.

He listened patiently and then said something simple: “Sir, this will slow us down during peak hours.”

That one sentence changed the direction of the system.

Ground Reality vs Designed Reality

From a system perspective, barcode scanning ensures accuracy. From an operational perspective, the reality was different.

During peak dispatch hours:

  • Multiple trucks were loaded simultaneously
  • Workers moved quickly to meet timelines
  • Internet connectivity was inconsistent in certain areas
  • Time pressure was high

Stopping to scan each item was not just inconvenient. It was impractical. If we had ignored this insight, the system would have been:

✔ Technically sound     ❌ Operationally rejected

Instead, we redesigned the workflow. We introduced:

  • Batch-level validation instead of item-level scanning in peak scenarios
  • Offline capture with later sync
  • Exception-based scanning for critical items
  • Flexible workflows depending on load volume

The system became slightly more complex technically. But significantly more usable operationally. And that made all the difference.

The Hidden Gap in Most Software Projects

Most digital systems are designed in meeting rooms. But they are used in environments that are far less controlled:

  • Noisy shop floors
  • Time-sensitive dispatch zones
  • Unpredictable field conditions
  • High-pressure operational settings

When systems are designed without understanding this reality, a gap emerges. A gap between Designed workflow and Actual behavior.

This gap leads to:

  • Workarounds
  • Partial adoption
  • Data inconsistencies
  • Frustration among teams
And eventually: “System is good, but practically it doesn’t work.”

Another Lesson From a Plant Supervisor

In a manufacturing setup, we were building a production tracking system. The objective was clear: Track every stage of production digitally.

The design included:

  • Step-by-step process updates
  • Real-time status tracking
  • Supervisor approvals at each stage

It looked structured and efficient. During a plant visit, we discussed the workflow with a supervisor. He said something interesting:

“If I have to update the system after every step, I will spend more time on the screen than on production.”

That insight revealed a critical flaw. The system assumed that data capture frequency equals control.

But in reality, excessive data entry reduces productivity. So we redesigned:

  • Critical checkpoints instead of every step
  • Automated status progression based on triggers
  • Minimal manual input

The result? Better data accuracy. Higher adoption. Less resistance.

Ground Reality Shapes Better Software

These experiences reinforced a powerful belief:

The best systems are not designed from assumptions. They are shaped by observation.

Operators don’t speak in technical terms. They don’t talk about APIs or architectures.

But they understand:

  • Where delays happen
  • Where errors occur
  • What slows down work
  • What actually matters

Their insights are practical, immediate, and often brutally honest. And that honesty is what makes systems better.

Respecting Operational Knowledge

One of the biggest mindset shifts for technology teams is this: Expertise does not always sit at the top.

In fact, in many cases:

  • Executives define direction
  • Managers define structure
  • Operators define reality

Ignoring any one of these layers creates imbalance. Respecting operational knowledge means:

  • Listening without bias
  • Observing workflows without assumptions
  • Validating designs against real scenarios
  • Accepting that theory may not always work in practice

The Emotional Side of Being Heard

There is another dimension to this. When operators are involved in system design:

  • They feel valued
  • They feel respected
  • They feel ownership

And ownership drives adoption. When systems are imposed from the top, resistance is natural. But when systems are shaped with input from the ground, adoption becomes easier. Because people don’t feel like they are using someone else’s system. They feel like they are using their system.

The Infipre Learning

Over time, one principle has become central to how we approach projects: The closer we get to the ground reality, the better the solution becomes. This is why we emphasize:

  • On-site visits
  • Process observation
  • Operator-level discussions
  • Real-world scenario testing

Not as a formality. But as a necessity. Because no amount of documentation can replace first-hand understanding.

A Different Definition of Expertise

We often associate expertise with experience, titles, and strategic thinking.

But there is another kind of expertise. The kind that comes from doing the same task every day. The kind that understands:

  • Where shortcuts happen
  • Where errors are tolerated
  • Where processes break under pressure

This expertise may not always be articulated in formal language. But it is incredibly valuable.

Final Thought

Technology can be designed in isolation. But it cannot succeed in isolation. The systems that truly work are not just technically strong. They are operationally aligned.

And that alignment comes from listening — not just to leadership, but to the people who live the process every day. Because sometimes, the most powerful insights don’t come from strategy decks. They come from a simple sentence on the shop floor:

“Sir, this won’t work here.”

And if you listen carefully enough, that sentence might just save your entire project.
